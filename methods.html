Foundational questions

Objects, relationships, and physical exchange.

What is a physical object?

We often begin by identifying a system, defining its properties, and then studying its interactions. ILC investigates whether this order of explanation conceals something important: the relationships and exchanges through which systems interact may help establish the properties by which those systems become distinguishable.

An object may be understood as a relatively stable organization of relationships and exchanges. Its boundaries describe conditions through which it remains identifiable, interacts with its surroundings, and changes.

A boundary may carry geometry or physical degrees of freedom, regulate material and energetic exchange, constrain information, or change through the very interactions it mediates. Mathematical domain boundaries, physical interfaces, changes of representation, and limits of continuation each require their own precise descriptions.

We investigate what remains identifiable through these relationships, what is conserved or transformed, and what conditions allow organized physical systems to persist. Across different research programs, those questions provide opportunities to discover whether common principles govern genuinely different systems and scales.

Why research develops in series

Follow the question through its consequences.

An individual paper can establish a particular mathematical or physical relationship. A research series can investigate what happens to that relationship when the same construction must satisfy additional conditions.

At each stage, established definitions, mathematical structures, and physical data become inherited foundations for the next question. New requirements may reveal information preserved across a representation, a previously unseen constraint, an exchange law at a physical interface, a counterexample, or an obstruction to continuation.

The continuity of the investigation makes those outcomes intelligible. Each paper contributes an established result and a precise place from which further inquiry can proceed. The connections between papers document how a scientific question develops, which assumptions carry forward, and what becomes possible next.

To discover what governs a physical system, we investigate what remains true as its constituent structures interact, exchange, and change.

Continuing discovery

Research that supports continuing discovery.

Scientific knowledge becomes more useful when its definitions, assumptions, methods, and limitations remain available for examination and further development.

ILC develops research with this continuity in mind. Mathematical precision, formal verification, and reproducible computational methods allow investigators working across different disciplines to engage with a common scientific foundation.

As artificial intelligence becomes increasingly capable of supporting scientific investigation, this approach also creates opportunities for collaboration between human researchers and computational systems. The standards of mathematical proof, independent verification, and empirical evidence remain essential throughout.
